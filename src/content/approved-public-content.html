Four ways value moves

Substitute

Which profits are at risk?

AI competes with businesses paid to think or coordinate. The risk is owning the work that becomes cheap to copy.

Amplify

What does cheap intelligence need?

More AI use needs more power, machines and verification. I look for suppliers that retain pricing power as use grows.

Reprice

What cannot be made abundant?

Land, rights, attention and scarce monetary assets remain limited. I look for owners that capture the extra value.

Unlock

What becomes a viable business?

Cheaper cognition makes new services and markets viable. The next generation of companies emerges in these markets.

A living map of the post-AI economy

When intelligence collapses in price, value doesn't stay in the intelligence layer. It leaves what can be reproduced, flows to the complements and the non-reproducible, and pools in categories that only become viable once cognition is nearly free. The essays follow it, with named companies, real figures, and the conditions that would make the argument wrong.

The time path Buildout · Digestion · Diffusion
Phase I · now

Buildout

≈ 2025–27
What happens
The physical substrate goes up faster than it can be supplied. Power, grid, cooling and silicon become the binding constraints, and the constraint sets the price.
Where the value sits
With whoever owns the bottleneck. Scarcity earns the rent, not the software.
Rhymes with
  • Canal Mania, Britain 1790–93
  • US railroad boom 1866–73
  • Long-haul fibre buildout 1996–2000
Phase II · next

Digestion

≈ 2027–30
What happens
The question turns from "can it be built" to "did it pay." Returns on the buildout are tested in public and financing gets selective. This is where the reset lives.
Where the value sits
With the survivors, and with whoever still holds cash. Most of the durable positions of the next decade are bought here.
Rhymes with
  • Canal share collapse 1793–97
  • Railroad panic, then six lean years 1873–79
  • Telecom bust 2000–02
Phase III · later

Diffusion

≈ 2030–35
What happens
The capability stops being the story and becomes the substrate. It shows up in ordinary companies' margins rather than in vendors' revenue.
Where the value sits
With rails, trust, land and attention, and with the categories that exist only because cognition got cheap.
Rhymes with
  • Cheap bulk transport feeds the factory economy
  • National distribution, then mail-order retail
  • Dark fibre becomes broadband, cloud, streaming

Each phase pays a different owner. Getting the sequence wrong is the most expensive way to be right about the destination.

What the essays go after
  1. Which complements cheap intelligence consumes, and how long their scarcity actually holds before supply responds.
  2. What intelligence cannot reproduce, and what happens to the price of it when everything else gets cheap.
  3. Which businesses only become possible once cognition is nearly free, and which of them can hold a moat.
  4. Where control survives the collapse, and where the moat is already gone and the market hasn't noticed.
  5. What would make all of this wrong: a capability plateau, adoption that stalls, or the labs capturing the complements themselves.

The order changes as the evidence arrives. The questions are the fixed part.

01 / Portfolio turnaround · 2014–2017

Head of Business Unit

The constraint

Profitable programmes masked losses elsewhere. Separate functional departments meant nobody owned the full economics of a client.

What I changed

03 / Scale-up operating model · 2019–2021

DEGIRO

Product & Delivery Transformation Lead

3 teams → 12 squadsIn 3 tribes, with Product Owners

Development organisation ~30 → 60–70 people

The constraint

Founder-led coordination no longer matched the demands of a growing online broker. Business demand and technology capacity had become disconnected.

What I changed

Built formal business reviews, capacity quotas and one prioritised backlog. Restructured development into 12 cross-functional squads across three tribes and, while the new leadership structure was being staffed, operated at tribe/GPM level across product direction, prioritisation, capacity allocation and cross-squad decisions.

What changed

A defined interface between business priorities and delivery capacity replaced ad hoc coordination as the development organisation grew.

A lesson carried forward: operating design needs sponsor alignment and a coalition able to sustain the change.

Tinkoff Insurance · 2013–2014Online sales live in five months

Greenfield under hard constraints. A new insurer’s core platform on a roughly $7M fixed-price programme. The binding constraint was scarce Guidewire expertise, so I built a ~30-person core team around the limited experienced talent available and ran inception as one team with the client and Guidewire Professional Services.

Online sales went live five months after implementation start, followed by phone sales a month later and further releases during the year. The fixed-price programme then became a continuing time-and-materials relationship as the client internalised the operating model.

Fortune Global 500 bank · 2010–2013Programme recovery · ~30,000 users

A stalled multi-vendor programme where the problem was the system, not the technology. Business units, internal IT, a prime contractor and our team were working under fixed-price commitments without a shared production cycle connecting demand to delivery.

I designed a common release model with fixed dates, capacity rules, prioritisation, entry criteria and explicit obligations for every party. Delivery became predictable, the team grew from ~50 to ~100, the programme reached ~30,000 users, the engagement economics recovered, and the client later absorbed the operating model into its own technology organisation.

Large retail bank · 2014–2015Turnaround through a portfolio leader

Transformation through others. The largest programme in my portfolio, representing roughly a third of portfolio revenue, was loss-making and had a dissatisfied client. With the portfolio manager, I diagnosed the problem and designed the recovery model and the case for changing how the programme operated.

I joined the critical executive discussions as the authority, but the portfolio manager owned execution. The programme recovered. For me, the important result was that the approach had become an organisational capability rather than something that depended on me personally.

How I work

Understand the constraint.
Change the system producing it.

The work connects economic diagnosis, sponsor alignment and execution.
The outcome must be visible in how the business performs.

01 / Make it legible

Find the real constraint

Where are value and margin created? Which assumptions no longer hold? Make the economics, ownership and dependencies explicit.

02 / Align the mandate

Connect authority to accountability

Agree the outcome with the sponsor. Establish decision rights, resources and escalation paths. Build the coalition with management.

03 / Redesign & execute

Change what drives the result

Reset priorities, capacity, incentives, leadership or the operating model. I lead the critical changes with the management team.

04 / Measure & embed

Make the outcome durable

Test results against the economic baseline, adjust what is not working and build the capability to sustain the improvement.

Accountability needs enough authority over the decisions and dependencies that produce the result.

Advisory & operating mandates

The problem sets the agenda.
The engagement follows.

I work best when there is:

  • Meaningful economic value at stake and a problem worth understanding properly.
  • An aligned executive sponsor who genuinely owns the outcome.
  • Accountability matched by real decision authority.
  • Willingness to change people, structures or assumptions.
  • A measurable outcome, with an agreed baseline and review points.
Start
A focused diagnostic can establish the economic baseline, value thesis and priority interventions. An Asset Legibility Review typically takes 3–4 weeks.
Form
Strategic advisory, an interim or fractional operating mandate, or a longer executive engagement. The form follows the problem and the authority required.
Duration
Change mandates typically run 3–9 months. Longer commitments can fit when the strategic challenge evolves, decision authority remains real and economic participation is meaningful.
